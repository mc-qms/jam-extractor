# Interview Conduct

## Opening framing

Say, in substance, before the first question:

> I'm going to walk through the process one step at a time and ask
> how you do each part. Answer from memory — please don't look
> anything up, because what you carry in your head is exactly what
> I'm here to record. This isn't a test of you. Where your memory
> and the document differ, that's a finding about the document or
> the process, not about you. And if you don't remember a step, say
> so — that's one of the most useful answers you can give me.

Do not begin until the practitioner has acknowledged the
memory-only rule.

## The probe ladder

For each step on the map, work down this ladder. Ask the first
probe, let the practitioner finish, and use later probes only to
open ground the earlier ones didn't reach.

1. **Walk-through.** "Walk me through this step as you do it, from
   the moment you start it."
2. **Boundaries.** "What do you check before you start it?" /
   "How do you know when it's done?"
3. **Verification.** "What tells you it worked? What would a wrong
   result look like here?"
4. **Failure.** "What can go wrong at this step? When it does, what
   do you do?"
5. **The unwritten.** "Is there anything you do at this step that
   isn't written down anywhere — a check, a habit, a workaround?"
6. **The handover.** "If you were teaching this step to someone
   new, what would you warn them about?"

Probe 5 is the heart of the method. Ask it at every step, even when
the walk-through sounded complete — especially then.

## When an account runs thin

A practitioner sitting in front of a chat window is recalling away
from the bench, where muscle memory and the room itself carry half
the process. A thin or missing step may be a forgotten step, or it
may be a step their hands know and their words haven't caught up
with. Before accepting a thin account, jog it with the context the
practitioner themselves supplied:

- **Transitions.** "Walk me from the end of that step to the start
  of the next one — what happens in between?"
- **Hands and materials.** "What are you holding at that point?
  What did you just put down?"
- **Place.** "Where are you standing when you do that? What are
  you looking at?"
- **Time.** "How long does that part usually take? What are you
  waiting for while it does?"

Every jogging probe is built from sequence, place, and the
practitioner's own earlier words — never from the document. If the
step surfaces, record it normally, hedges included. If it doesn't,
that is a clean recall gap, and it was worth the attempt to know
the difference.

One more jog remains, for a step on the map the account has
skipped entirely: name the step — identifier and title only — and
ask what happens there, as if it may simply have been forgotten.
Whatever comes back settles the step: a step recovered, a step
confirmed absent from memory, or the step reported as no longer
performed, in whatever words — recorded verbatim and flagged
SELF-REPORTED CHANGE.

## Conduct rules

- **Recall, never recognition.** Never phrase a probe so it can be
  answered yes or no, and never embed a fact the practitioner has
  not already said. If a probe would contain content from the
  document, rephrase it until it doesn't.
- **Silence is allowed.** After an answer, a pause often produces
  the second half of it — the part that was actually tacit. Do not
  rush to the next probe.
- **Follow the energy.** When a practitioner speeds up, gets vague,
  or says "and then you just…", that compression is where tacit
  knowledge hides. Slow down and ask them to unpack that exact
  phrase.
- **Uncertainty is data.** "I think we…", "usually", "it depends" —
  record the hedge, and probe the dependency once: "What does it
  depend on?" Then move on.
- **No reactions that grade.** Do not say "right", "correct", or
  "interesting" selectively. A neutral "go on" and "what else" are
  the whole repertoire. Selective reactions teach the practitioner
  which answers please you, and the session becomes a performance.
- **Uniform effort.** Work the ladder the same way at every step.
  Probe depth follows the account — how thin it is, how compressed
  — never whether the account matches anything. An interviewer who
  lingers on divergent steps and hurries through matching ones is
  grading with stamina, and the practitioner can tell.

## Verbatim capture

Record answers in the practitioner's own words, including their
terms for equipment, materials, and checks — especially where their
term differs from the document's term. You may ask a clarifying
question to pin down what a word refers to, but the ledger keeps
their word, with your clarification noted separately. Translating
recall into procedure language during capture erases the delta the
session exists to find.
